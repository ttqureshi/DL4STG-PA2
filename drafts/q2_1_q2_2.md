# Q2.1 and Q2.2 (draft)

Figure 1 is Farneback optical flow on the first 50 training videos, dataset indices 0–49. That is 950 consecutive frame pairs. A pixel is kept when its intensity is above 0.2 in both frames and the flow magnitude is above 0.5. The figure uses 103,050 kept pixels. The median speed is 3.32 pixels/frame. Direction is θ = atan2(−dy, dx) in degrees on [0°, 360°), with 0° rightward and 90° upward. The dashed line in panel (a) is the uniform level 1/18 ≈ 0.0556.

## Q2.1

Mirrored and rotated trajectories are only partly familiar. Every 20° bin in Figure 1(a) already contains some kept pixels, so neither transform introduces a compass direction that is absent from this sample. The histogram is uneven, and it changes under both transforms, so the balance of directions is not something this training sample has already shown.

The bins are not close to uniform. The shortest is [120°, 140°), up and to the left, with fraction 0.0263 (2.63% of kept pixels, 0.474 times the uniform level). The tallest is [180°, 200°), just past leftward, with fraction 0.0820 (8.20%, 1.48 times uniform). The next peaks are [340°, 360°), rightward and slightly down, at 0.0772 (7.72%), and [60°, 80°), up and to the right, at 0.0731 (7.31%). Two further short bins are [140°, 160°) at 0.0326 and [300°, 320°) at 0.0366. The tallest bin is 3.11 times the shortest. Mass sits all around the circle, with a real excess on the left, on the near-right, and up-right, and a real deficit up-left.

A horizontal mirror replaces (dx, dy) with (−dx, dy), which sends θ to 180° − θ. On these same kept pixels the leftward peak [180°, 200°) moves to [340°, 360°), and the near-rightward peak moves the other way. The scarce up-left sector [120°, 160°) lands in [20°, 60°). In the original histogram that destination is above the uniform line: [40°, 60°) has fraction 0.0675. Turning the original direction histogram into the mirrored one reassigns 13.2% of kept pixels to a different 20° bin. Mirrored videos still use directions that occur in training. They do not use them in the proportions Figure 1(a) records.

A 90° anticlockwise rotation adds 90° to every direction. Because 90° is 4.5 bin widths, each 20° bin splits across two bins rather than sliding onto one bin. On these pixels the tallest rotated bin is [280°, 300°), just past downward, at 0.0858, and the shortest is [200°, 220°) at 0.0290. The leftward excess moves toward down. Turning the original histogram into the rotated one reassigns 20.0% of kept pixels. Rotated trajectories are in the same position as mirrored ones: every direction already appears, and the histogram of which directions dominate does not.

Figure 1(b) puts a typical speed at the median, 3.32 pixels/frame. The interquartile range is 3.00 to 3.89. The bin [3.0, 3.5) holds 0.276 of kept pixels and [3.5, 4.0) holds 0.262, so 0.538 of the sample lies between 3 and 4 pixels/frame. The interval [2.5, 4.5) holds 0.835. The 99th percentile is 4.67 pixels/frame, and the share faster than 5 pixels/frame is 0.00465.

Speed-up by k keeps every k-th frame. At constant velocity the displacement between successive frames that remain is k times the consecutive-frame displacement. Twice the measured median is 6.64 pixels/frame, and three times it is 9.95 pixels/frame. Both are outside the body of Figure 1(b).

Overlap can bias both histograms, and it is unlikely to change these answers. Where two digits occupy the same pixels, Farneback is not measuring one digit’s velocity, so some of the 103,050 kept pixels are spurious. Those pixels can scatter across direction bins and can add a few very fast vectors. The speed histogram is too concentrated for that tail to move the scale: 0.835 of kept pixels lie between 2.5 and 4.5 pixels/frame, and only 0.00465 exceed 5. A minority of overlap vectors does not move the median off 3.32, and it does not bring 6.64 or 9.95 into the main mass. On direction, overlap could reorder the close peaks at 0.0820, 0.0772, and 0.0731. Filling the gap between 0.0263 and 0.0820, or making the histogram flat, would take a much larger share of the sample than a passing overlap. The claims that remain are the ones above: motion occurs in every 20° sector with a moderate imbalance, mirror and a 90° rotation both reshuffle that imbalance (13.2% and 20.0% of pixels change bins), and a typical speed is 3.32 pixels/frame.

## Q2.2

Mirror changes the digit appearance and the direction statistics. The map (Mx)[i, j] = x[i, 63 − j] reflects the glyph, so a mirrored digit is a different character, and it reflects flow by θ → 180° − θ. Figure 1(a) changes under that reflection, as Q2.1 measures. Speed does not change: √(dx² + dy²) is unchanged when dx changes sign, so the speed histogram and the median of 3.32 pixels/frame stay put.

Rotation changes the digit appearance and the direction statistics. A 90° anticlockwise turn changes the glyph further than a mirror does, and it adds 90° to every flow angle. Figure 1(a) changes under that shift (20.0% of kept pixels move bins). Speed is unchanged, because a rotation preserves the flow magnitude. The median remains 3.32 pixels/frame.

Speed-up changes the motion statistics and leaves the digit appearance as it is. The frames that remain are original frames, so each glyph is the same image. Keeping every second or third frame multiplies the step between successive observed frames by two or three when velocity is constant. Relative to the measured median, that is 6.64 pixels/frame at 2× and 9.95 pixels/frame at 3×. Figure 1(b) shows that consecutive-frame flow in this sample almost never moves that fast. Direction is not rescaled; the speed distribution is.

The three edits are a controlled experiment because they separate appearance, direction, and speed, and Figure 1 says which of those the training motion already contains. Speed-up holds the digit images fixed and moves speed to 6.64 or 9.95 pixels/frame, outside the consecutive-frame distribution whose median is 3.32. Mirror and rotation hold that speed fixed and change the glyph, and each applies one known map to direction: reflection, or a quarter turn. Those two maps are different, and Q2.1 shows both of them change Figure 1(a). Appearance, direction, and speed can therefore be changed one grouping at a time on the same videos.
